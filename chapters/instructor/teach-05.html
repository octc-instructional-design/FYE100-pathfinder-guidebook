Module 5: Financial Planning with Purpose

Slides, coaching notes, active learning ideas, and an in-class discussion.

Slides, coaching notes, and in-class ideas for Module 5: Financial Planning with Purpose.

At a glance

  • I can build a realistic zero-based budget that assigns all available income to specific categories.
  • I can identify at least one pattern in my spending habits and describe one small change I want to make.
  • I can explain what financial wellness means and describe how it connects to my overall wellbeing.
  • I can use AI tools safely for budgeting exploration without sharing personal financial information.

Coaching notes

Where students tend to get stuck this module, and what helps.

  • Set the privacy ground rule early. Money is personal, and some students are dealing with real hardship. Say it plainly in class and in your announcement: no assignment in this module asks for dollar amounts, and nobody is judged for their situation.
  • Question 2 is the hardest to write. Students are asked to describe their financial situation honestly without numbers. Some write too little; a few overshare. Model the level of detail: "I work part-time and cover my own gas and phone; anything unexpected is hard" is enough.
  • Money leaks are the student's call. Lesson 5.3 defines a leak as spending that doesn't match what the student values. Avoid feedback that judges choices (coffee, gaming, gifts). Ask whether it matches their priorities instead.
  • Question 5 rewards privacy habits. The strongest answers say what the student left out of their AI prompts and how they described their situation in general terms. "I didn't use AI" is a complete answer.
  • Financial Aid questions will come to you. The Trail Tip names two numbers that protect aid: a 2.0 GPA and a 67% completion rate. Students often don't know that a withdrawal counts against completion. Send schedule-change questions to Financial Aid before students act.
  • Money stress can be a warning sign. If a student mentions they can't afford food, housing, or to stay enrolled, follow up privately and connect them with their Success Coach and the money help listed in Lesson 5.2.
  • The Inventory matters to the course. The Appreciative Advising Inventory is completion only, but students' answers shape the course. Tell students what changed last time because of it; the Results in Action page in this guide has the details.

Active learning ideas

Pick one or two. Each fits in a 50-minute class alongside the slides.

Leak or priority?

Time: 10 minutesYou need: A list of eight to ten everyday purchases on the screen

  1. Show purchases like a streaming service, a delivery fee, a gym membership, gas, a gift, and a late fee.
  2. Students decide for each one, privately: leak or priority, for them?
  3. Discuss: did everyone agree? Why not? Connect it to the lesson's definition of a leak.

Why it works: Shows that a leak depends on what each person values, and gives students language for Mile Marker #5, Question 1.

Online sections: Post the list as an anonymous poll and share the spread of answers in your next announcement.

Give every dollar a job

Time: 15 minutesYou need: A sample monthly income and expenses for a fictional student, plus the Budget Builder

  1. In pairs, students build a zero-based budget for the fictional student until every dollar has a job.
  2. Introduce a surprise: a $150 car repair. What changes?
  3. Debrief: what made the surprise easier or harder to absorb?

Why it works: Practices the Budget Builder with numbers that aren't anyone's own, so nobody has to share.

Online sections: Share the fictional student's numbers and the surprise expense, and suggest students try it before building their own.

The two numbers

Time: 5 minutesYou need: The Financial Aid Trail Tip on the screen

  1. Ask students to guess the two numbers that protect financial aid before showing them.
  2. Reveal the 2.0 GPA and 67% completion rate, and explain how a withdrawal affects completion.
  3. Ask: who would you contact before dropping a class?

Why it works: A quick, high-stakes fact many students learn too late.

Online sections: Put the two numbers in an announcement this week, with the Financial Aid contact.

In-class discussion

The money tip you wish you'd known

  1. What's one money tip you wish you'd learned sooner?
  2. Did someone teach you, or did you learn it the hard way?

Talk with a partner for 3 minutes, then we'll build a class top-ten list.

Where it comes from: An in-class version of the online discussion: What's One Money Tip You Really Wish You'd Learned Sooner?

Facilitating: Set the ground rule first: no numbers, and tips are enough. If a student shares hardship, thank them, keep it moving, and follow up privately if needed.
